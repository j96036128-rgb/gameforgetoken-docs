# Good Problems — Open Questions in Coordination

These are unresolved, non-trivial questions facing GameForge and coordination systems generally.

They are not promises, tasks, or commitments. They are not a backlog. No one is assigned to solve them.

They exist to invite thinking, critique, and experimentation.

---

## 1. Scaling Review Without Centralisation

**The question:** How do you increase review capacity without creating a small group that controls all acceptance decisions?

**Why it's hard:** Review requires judgment. Judgment requires trust. Trust takes time to build. As submissions increase, the people who have earned trust become bottlenecks. Adding more reviewers dilutes quality control. Not adding them creates a chokepoint.

**Why it matters:** If review doesn't scale, the system stops accepting work or accepts work carelessly. Either outcome undermines the ledger's credibility.

**Failure mode:** A handful of reviewers become de facto gatekeepers. Contributors route around them or stop participating. The system becomes a rubber stamp or a dead end.

---

## 2. Preventing Soft Capture Without Freezing Governance

**The question:** How do you prevent a small group from accumulating disproportionate influence while still allowing governance to evolve?

**Why it's hard:** Coordination systems reward consistency. People who show up repeatedly gain standing. Over time, early participants accumulate trust, context, and relationships that newcomers lack. Freezing governance protects against capture but prevents adaptation. Allowing change creates opportunities for capture.

**Why it matters:** Capture doesn't require malice. Well-intentioned people can centralise power without noticing. If the system cannot self-correct, it becomes whatever its most persistent participants want it to be.

**Failure mode:** A stable inner circle emerges. Newcomers cannot reach parity. The system claims to be open but functions as a club.

---

## 3. Measuring Contribution Quality Without Metrics

**The question:** How do you distinguish high-quality work from adequate work without introducing scores, rankings, or quantified evaluation?

**Why it's hard:** Metrics are legible and comparable. Judgment is opaque and inconsistent. Without metrics, "quality" becomes whatever reviewers say it is. With metrics, contributors optimise for numbers rather than outcomes.

**Why it matters:** If all accepted work is treated equally, there's no signal about what "good" looks like. If quality is quantified, gaming follows.

**Failure mode:** Either the system cannot recognise excellence, or it creates a leaderboard mentality that poisons collaboration.

---

## 4. When (and If) Tokenisation Improves Coordination

**The question:** Under what specific conditions does adding a token improve coordination outcomes rather than distort them?

**Why it's hard:** Tokens introduce price signals. Price signals attract speculators. Speculators change who participates and why. The presence of a token changes the system even if the token has no direct function. Proving that a token helped requires a counterfactual that cannot be observed.

**Why it matters:** Premature tokenisation has destroyed coordination systems. So has refusing to tokenise when scale demanded it. The decision is high-stakes and largely irreversible.

**Failure mode:** Tokenise too early: attract extractors, lose builders. Tokenise too late: lose contributors to systems that reward participation. Never tokenise: remain small and fragile.

---

## 5. Sunsetting Authority Without Destabilising Systems

**The question:** How does a founder or initial authority step back without creating a power vacuum or triggering a succession crisis?

**Why it's hard:** Early systems depend on trusted individuals to resolve ambiguity. Removing that trust before the system can handle ambiguity on its own causes paralysis. Keeping it too long creates dependency. There is no clean handoff point.

**Why it matters:** Systems that cannot outlive their founders are projects, not protocols. Systems that transfer power badly become battlegrounds.

**Failure mode:** Founder leaves too early: system fragments. Founder stays too long: system ossifies around them. Succession is contested: system splits.

---

## 6. Maintaining Momentum During Quiet Periods

**The question:** How does a coordination system stay credible and functional when activity is low?

**Why it's hard:** Coordination systems require participants. Participants require visible activity. Low activity signals abandonment. Signaling abandonment drives away remaining participants. Artificial activity is transparent and erodes trust.

**Why it matters:** All systems have quiet periods. The ones that survive them do so without pretending they're busy.

**Failure mode:** Activity drops. Remaining contributors assume the system is dead. System becomes dead because everyone assumed it was.

---

## 7. Balancing Transparency With Operational Efficiency

**The question:** How much process visibility is necessary for legitimacy, and when does transparency become overhead that slows coordination?

**Why it's hard:** Transparency builds trust. Trust enables action. But documenting everything takes time. Reading documentation takes time. At some point, the system spends more effort proving it's fair than doing work.

**Why it matters:** Opaque systems are captured easily. Fully transparent systems are slow and exhausting. The right balance is context-dependent and changes over time.

**Failure mode:** Too opaque: decisions feel arbitrary, contributors disengage. Too transparent: every action requires justification, contributors burn out.

---

## 8. Defining Scope Boundaries for Domain Trust

**The question:** How do you grant authority in one domain without it bleeding into others?

**Why it's hard:** Expertise is specific. Trust is general. A contributor trusted to review frontend code may be asked to review protocol design. Saying "no" feels like an insult. Saying "yes" degrades review quality. Formal domain boundaries create bureaucracy. Informal ones create ambiguity.

**Why it matters:** As the system grows, specialisation becomes necessary. Without clear scope boundaries, generalists dominate and specialists leave.

**Failure mode:** Domain boundaries are ignored: unqualified reviews proliferate. Domain boundaries are rigid: contributors feel boxed in and leave.

## Rules

- Anyone may propose additions via GitHub Issue
- Proposals should follow the format above: question, why it's hard, why it matters, failure mode
- No expectation of solution — problems can remain open indefinitely
- Disagreement is welcome — if you think a problem is poorly framed, say so
- This document is advisory, not authoritative — it does not create obligations or commitments
